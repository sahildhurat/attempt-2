When Memory Runs Out Mid-Search

The brief asks how people search when their memory is incomplete. That population can be isolated exactly: 51 of 720 units carry both a remembered cue and a forgotten one — someone who knew something, and not enough. Splitting them is the most useful thing in this study.

knew the photo, couldn't drive the app
24
still remembered it, had lost the handle
21
system lost or changed it
3
not a retrieval cue
3

Nearly half are not memory failures at all. They are people who know exactly which photo they want and cannot find the control — the sort option, the hidden-faces list, the locked folder. Counting those as a memory problem would overstate it by about two to one.

The 21 that remain are the ones worth building for, and they have a shape. Thirteen of the twenty-one still held a content description — they could say what was in the photograph. What had gone was the handle: the date, the filename, which app made it, where it had been saved.

Held: location where photo was taken — Gone: when the photo was taken
Held: it was a screen recording — Gone: the filename of the recording
Held: event was an award ceremony; who gave the award — Gone: which of many Drives it was saved in

Every one of those people could have described the photo to a friend. None of them could give a search box the one token it wanted. Search indexes handles; memory keeps descriptions. That gap is the problem this project exists to close, and it is 21 units wide in this corpus — small, specific, and under-served rather than large and vague.

Where the Opportunity Is

Putting the two distributions side by side answers the brief's last question. The cue people hold most often is the one the system handles worst. Content description is the single most common thing a person remembers — and text and keyword search is the single most common way the search fails them.

What they bring to the searchnWhat happens to itn
content description140text and keyword search fails37
date and time94date and time search fails — dates are treated as soft keywords, not filters11
person identity87face and people search fails8
location32location search fails or is unavailable2

Two opportunity areas fall out of that. First, the describable-but-unsearchable gap: people arrive holding a description and the system can only match keywords, so 57% of targets are a describable kind of photo while the commonest failure is description-shaped. Second, the hedged-detail gap: dates and places are remembered often and imprecisely, and a system that treats them as hard filters rejects the very users who are closest to the answer.

Counts as published above; the two columns are independent taxonomies over the same 720 units, not a measured causal link.

Limitations

  • Gate over-inclusion at 56% precision so counts are upper bounds.
  • The cue field was 43% contaminated and corrected.
  • The forgotten set was re-coded AI-proposed and human-reviewed, not independently validated.
  • Temperature could not be set to 0 so induction variance conflates shuffle order with sampling.
  • Breakdown, workaround and target taxonomies were AI-consolidated without a human checkpoint, unlike cues.
  • iOS underrepresented at 2.4% gate yield.
  • Public forum text self-selects for people who failed and sought help.
  • Source Coverage: The brief names social media and YouTube comments among its example sources and neither was collected. The five source types used (Google Help Community, Stack Exchange, Hacker News, App Store, Play Store) mean the corpus skews toward written help-seeking rather than conversational commentary.
