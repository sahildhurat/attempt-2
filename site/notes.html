How the corpus was built

41,038 units were collected and 41,006 survived de-duplication. A stratified 7,840 went through the relevance gate, and 720 were fully extracted. Collection was broad; what survived was not. Three thousand Play Store reviews produced a single usable unit, because a star rating is not a place people narrate a failed search. They narrate it where they are asking for help.

The prompts, verbatim

Each pass ran the prompt below unchanged across the whole stratum. Nothing was hand-edited per unit.

Pass 1 — relevance gate
# System Prompt\nYou are a classifier. Your task is to determine whether a piece of user feedback\nis about someone trying to find a specific photo or video they believe already\nexists in their Google Photos library.\n\n# Decision procedure\n\nApply these steps in order.\n\nSTEP 1 — Is the person trying to reach a photo or video they believe exists in\ntheir own Google Photos library?\n  If no, the decision is "no". This includes questions about the search feature\n  itself (its placement, how to disable it), general feature discussion,\n  problems on other platforms or other apps, and app issues where no attempt to\n  reach a photo is described.\n  Step 1 excludes a text only if NO attempt to reach a photo is described. A\n  bug report, complaint or feature question that ALSO describes someone\n  trying and failing to reach a photo passes step 1. The framing does not\n  matter; the attempt does.\n  If yes, continue to step 2.\n\nSTEP 2 — Do they name a specific target?\n  A specific target is a photo identified by its content, subject, event,\n  date, place, document type, or by a concrete query the person ran and what\n  they expected it to return. A browsing MODE is not a target — "photos in\n  reverse chronological order", "my uploaded photos", "everything from the\n  last ten years" fail step 2 and are "partial".\n  If yes, the decision is "yes".\n  If no, but they describe attempting retrieval, the decision is "partial".\n\nSTEP 3 — OVERRIDE. If the person states the photo or video was deleted, by them\nor anyone else, or asks about recovery or restoration, the decision is "no",\nregardless of steps 1 and 2.\n  Step 3 applies only when the person STATES the photo was deleted, or asks\n  how to recover or restore something they know was deleted. It does NOT\n  apply when they are asking whether photos were deleted, or cannot tell\n  whether an item is deleted or merely hidden. Uncertainty is not a statement\n  of deletion.\n\nNotes:\n- The CAUSE of the failure is not the criterion. Someone who cannot reach photos\n  they believe exist has a retrieval problem whether the cause is search, sync,\n  indexing or a display bug.\n- A person who describes a query they ran and what they expected back has named\n  a specific target, even if they give it as an example of a broader problem.\n- Inventory requests ("find all duplicates", "photos not in any album") pass\n  step 1 but fail step 2, so they are "partial".\n- Where the person does not know whether a photo is deleted or merely hidden,\n  step 3 does not apply — their searching behaviour is still evidence.\n\nGive your reason before your decision. Keep your reason brief (maximum 25 words).\n\n# Examples\n\n**Example 1**\nText: "I wrong delete vedio. I want recovery vedio"\nReason: User explicitly states they wrongly deleted a video and asks for recovery. Step 3 override applies.\nDecision: no\n\n**Example 2**\nText: "How to search and delete exact duplicate photos and videos"\nReason: User is asking about finding duplicates as a category, an inventory request. Fails step 2 as no specific target is named.\nDecision: partial\n\n**Example 3**\nText: "How to remove the search feature? I keep accidently clicking it..."\nReason: User asks how to disable the search feature itself, not trying to find a specific photo.\nDecision: no\n\n**Example 4**\nText: "where are all the shared pictures and videos? videos people shared are gone"\nReason: User is trying to find shared pictures and videos they believe exist. They name a specific target collection.\nDecision: yes\n\n# Special rule for replies\nIf this text is a reply to a relevant parent post, and this reply describes\nhow to find something (e.g., suggests a workaround, a search tip, or a\nmethod), classify it as "yes" — even if it does not restate the target photo.\n\n# Language\nIn addition, return the ISO 639-1 two-letter code for the language of the text in the `language` field (e.g. "en" for English, "es" for Spanish).
Pass 2 — extraction
You are an expert qualitative researcher analyzing user reports about Google Photos search. \n\nYour task is to extract exactly what the user was trying to retrieve (their target) and what specific search cues they remembered or forgot. \n\nSchema:\n```json\n{\n  "target": "A concise description of the photos they were trying to find",\n  "evidence_quote": "A single exact quote from the text supporting this extraction",\n  "remembered": [\n    {\n      "cue": "short phrase describing what they remembered",\n      "span": "exact verbatim substring of the source text supporting this cue"\n    }\n  ],\n  "forgotten": [\n    {\n      "cue": "short phrase describing what they could not remember",\n      "span": "exact verbatim substring of the source text supporting this cue"\n    }\n  ],\n  "workaround": "Any alternative method they used to find the photo when search failed",\n  "breakdown": "The reason the search failed, if stated"\n}\n```\n\nFor every item in "remembered" and "forgotten", you must supply a "span":\nan exact substring copied character-for-character from the source text that\nsupports that specific cue.\n\n- The span must appear verbatim in the text. Do not paraphrase, correct\n  spelling, expand abbreviations, or alter punctuation.\n- The span must be the SHORTEST verbatim substring that supports the cue —\n  maximum 20 words. Do not quote a whole sentence when a phrase suffices.\n- If you cannot find a verbatim span supporting a cue, do not output that cue\n  at all. An omitted cue is correct; an ungrounded cue is a failure.\n- Spans may overlap between cues. A single sentence can ground two cues.
Pass 3 — taxonomy induction
You are a qualitative researcher building a taxonomy from a list of short phrases.\n\nGroup the phrases below into categories derived from the phrases themselves, not from any prior expectation about what categories should exist. Each phrase belongs to at most one category.\n\nEach category must have:\n1. A short, descriptive name (1-3 words).\n2. A clear definition of what belongs in it.\n\nDo not create a catch-all category such as "Other" or "Miscellaneous". You need not force every phrase into a category — phrases that do not fit any coherent group may be left unassigned.\n\nPhrases:\n{phrases}

The taxonomies

Categories were induced by the model across four shuffled runs, consolidated by hand, and then frozen before any assignment was made. Each definition carries its exclusions, because the exclusions are what make an assignment repeatable.

What people remembered — cue taxonomy

9 categories · confirmed by Sahil

date and time

When the photo was taken: a date, month, year, season, or a life period. Examples: 'exact date of the photo', 'specific date taken'. NOT when it was uploaded, backed up or synced; NOT when a feature changed or stopped working.

location

Where the photo was taken: a place name, city, region, or a described place. Examples: 'location name - Pee Pee Township, Ohio', 'general location - New York City', 'location near a pier'. NOT where a file, folder or album sits inside the app; NOT the location of a setting or feature.

person identity

A specific individual in or connected to the photo, by name or relationship, including named pets. Examples: "specific person's name (e.g. Abigail)", 'which people are tagged (Hans, Julia)', 'photos were taken by husband', "dog's name (Anubis)". NOT how the app groups or tags faces; NOT a person described only as the photo's subject matter.

content description

What the photo shows: objects, animals, scenes, activities, or text visible inside the image. Examples: 'subject is sunrises', 'black and white dog in Hawaii', 'subject of photo (basketball goal)', 'exact text shown in the screenshot (KeroKero)'. NOT the named people in it; NOT where it was taken; NOT the occasion it belongs to.

occasion or event

The event or occasion the photo belongs to: a trip, wedding, birthday, festival, or a described happening. Examples: 'moments/events of kids and other relevant things'. NOT the generic subject matter of the image; NOT a bare date.

search term used

The literal word or phrase the person typed into search. Examples: "search keyword 'knife'", "search term 'golf'", "general subject keyword 'motorcycle'", "search term 'blowtorch'". This category is about the QUERY STRING ITSELF, not the attribute it refers to. A phrase belongs here only if it reports what was typed.

album or folder name

The name of an album or folder the photo sits in, as the person recalls it. Examples: 'album/folder name', 'which album the photo belongs to'. NOT whether albums exist, how many there are, how recent they are, or whether their contents changed.

file identity

Attributes of the file itself: its filename, extension, format, or size. Examples: 'filename with ABC_2022', "filename contains 'WA' from WhatsApp", 'specific words/strings contained in video filenames'. NOT the device that took it; NOT where it was uploaded from; NOT the photo's visual content.

capture source

The device, camera or app that produced the photo, or where it was uploaded from. Examples: 'camera/device name', 'which app generated it', 'upload source distinction - pixel XL vs laptop'. NOT the filename; NOT the account, album or folder it ended up in.

What they were looking for — target taxonomy

10 categories · confirmed by AI-consolidated (Claude), v2 single-axis

v2 replaces the v1 flat list, which mixed two dimensions (what the user wanted, and what was wrong) and produced 6290% duplicate consistency because many targets legitimately carried both. This version asks one question only: what kind of thing was the user trying to reach. The tie-break rule below resolves targets that also describe a problem.

Tie-break rule: If a target names BOTH a kind of photo AND a problem with it (missing, hidden, wrongly grouped, not syncing), choose the KIND OF PHOTO. The problem is already captured by the breakdown field. Choose 'not a specific photo' only when no kind of photo is identifiable at all.

a specific person

Photos of one or more named individuals, or of the user themselves, wanted because of who is in them. Includes people reached through face groups, and includes cases where those photos are missing or wrongly grouped. NOT a pet.

a pet or animal

Photos of a named pet or a particular animal the user owns or knows. NOT animals as a generic search subject such as 'dogs' or 'deer', which is an object or scene.

an object, scene or subject

Photos wanted for what they depict: an object, vehicle, food, landscape, colour, plant or activity. Examples: red cars, sunrises, a bee on a blue flower, a basketball goal. NOT a named person or pet, and NOT a document or text.

a document, ID or text

Photos taken to store information rather than to remember a moment: receipts, IDs, passwords, forms, labels, prescriptions, screenshots wanted for words or numbers in them. The photo is a record. NOT a screenshot wanted for its picture.

an event, occasion or trip

Photos belonging to a bounded happening: a wedding, birthday, holiday, walk, hospital visit. The event is the handle. NOT a bare date, and NOT the people who were present.

a place

Photos wanted because of where they were taken: a city, country, cabin, address or geotagged area. NOT an event that happened there, and NOT a feature of the app such as the map view.

media of a particular type

Photos or videos wanted for their format or capture mode: videos, motion or live photos, burst shots, RAW or DNG, HDR, screenshots as a class, WhatsApp images as a class. The format defines the set. NOT one individual file.

one particular photo, content not described

A single photo identified only by a filename, timestamp, caption the user added, or the fact that they once saw it, with nothing said about what it shows. Use only when no subject can be read from the target. NOT a class of files such as all videos or all screenshots, which is media of a particular type.

not a specific photo

The target is not a photo: a place in the app (Locked Folder, Sharing, an album, Memories, Recently Added), a feature, a setting, the search function itself, storage space, or a general complaint about behaviour. Use only when no kind of photo is identifiable. NOT photos that merely sit inside one of these places.

unspecified

The account does not say what the user was looking for. NOT a vague description that still names a subject or a format.

Where it broke, and what they did next — breakdown

12 categories · confirmed by AI-consolidated (Claude)

date and time search fails

Searching, filtering or sorting by date returns nothing, returns unrelated results, returns everything from a day instead of the target, or cannot be combined with another term. NOT photos sorted wrongly because their stored date is incorrect, which is metadata; NOT a date filter the product never offered.

text and keyword search fails

Words that are in, on or attached to the photo are not matched: object and scene keywords, text visible inside the image (OCR), filenames, and semantic or descriptive queries. Includes over-matching on partial tokens and short queries that stopped working after the AI search change. NOT text the user typed in themselves, which is its own category.

user-supplied metadata is ignored

Descriptions, captions, tags, filenames or timestamps the user added or edited are not matched by search, or revert. The user did the indexing work and the product did not use it. NOT metadata the camera or app wrote, which is the metadata category.

face and people search fails

Face detection, grouping or search by person does not work: new faces undetected, faces merged or split wrongly, named people not retrievable, groups lost. NOT a general content-recognition failure on objects or scenes.

location search fails or is unavailable

Search or filter by place returns incomplete results, has no name to match against, or the map view is unavailable on that platform. NOT a location the camera recorded incorrectly, which is metadata.

search returns an incomplete subset

Search finds some matching photos but visibly misses most of them, caps the result set, or cannot be expanded. The query is understood; the recall is wrong. NOT a search that returns nothing, and NOT one that returns the wrong photos entirely.

the capability does not exist

The product offers no mechanism for what the user wants: no tag support, no multi-criteria or exclusion operators, no duplicate detection, no reverse image search, no filtering to one media type. A missing feature. NOT a feature that exists but malfunctions, and NOT one the user simply could not find.

search terms are blocked or filtered

Specific query terms are censored or suppressed, returning zero results for ordinary content. NOT a term that simply fails to match anything in the library.

content not yet indexed or synced

Photos are absent from search because upload, backup or indexing has not completed, or backup is misconfigured. Transient or fixable by configuration. NOT photos that have been deleted or corrupted.

data lost or deleted

Photos are gone, corrupted, or unrecoverable, or deletion behaved unexpectedly across linked services. Permanent loss. NOT a sync or indexing delay that would resolve itself.

display and platform inconsistency

The data exists but the interface does not show it, shows it differently across web, Android and iOS, fails to display edited versions, or glitches while scrolling. A presentation failure. NOT a search that cannot find the photo, and NOT content that is genuinely absent.

user misunderstanding

The user misread a feature, setting or result and the product behaved as designed. Kept as a category so these cases can be excluded from product-gap counts rather than silently inflating them. NOT a confusing design that genuinely misleads, which belongs with the capability or display categories.

Where it broke, and what they did next — workaround

9 categories · confirmed by AI-consolidated (Claude)

scroll manually

Browsing the timeline, an album or search results by hand instead of searching, including bulk multi-select to act on what is found. The brute-force fallback. NOT using a filter or category to narrow the set first.

reformulate the query

Changing the search text itself: different wording, spelling variants, quotation marks, operators, or a much longer and more specific query. The route stays the same and only the words change. NOT switching to a filter, a category or another navigation route, and NOT editing the photo's own metadata.

try a different search route

Reaching the photo through another mechanism inside the app: category or attribute filters, date and Recently Added navigation, the location map, face groups, OCR text, visual or Lens search, or changing the result sort order. NOT changing the query text, and NOT leaving the app.

switch interface or device

Performing the same search somewhere else: the web client instead of the mobile app, Classic Search instead of the AI search, another phone or computer, or a different photo app on the same library. NOT exporting the files out.

build your own index

Making the photo findable by hand: adding descriptions, captions or tags, renaming files, correcting timestamps, creating albums or naming conventions, re-uploading to force reindexing, or keeping a record outside the app. The user doing the product's job. NOT adjusting app settings.

troubleshoot the app

Treating it as a technical fault: toggling settings or features off and on, reinstalling, forcing a re-sync, or waiting for background indexing to finish. NOT changing how the photo is searched for.

leave the app

Getting the photos out: Takeout, direct download, or migrating the library to another service. Abandoning retrieval inside Google Photos. NOT opening the same library in a different client, which is switching interface.

ask someone else

Turning to a person: contacting the original uploader, Google support, or a community forum to locate the photo. NOT keeping one's own external notes, which is building an index.

no workaround found

The account records no successful workaround: nothing was tried that worked, or the user is explicitly asking for a solution. The absence is the finding. NOT a workaround that was tried and failed, which is recorded under whichever tactic was attempted.

What this study cannot tell you

  • It is a complaint corpus. Everyone in it had a problem worth writing about. It says nothing about how often retrieval succeeds, and nothing about users who never post.
  • Short queries are over-represented. A one-word search is easier to quote verbatim than a long one, so the 60% figure is biased by the act of quoting. The mechanism is evidenced independently; the proportion is not.
  • The forgotten set is small. 61 cues against 554 remembered, because people narrate what they tried, not what had gone. Half of those turned out to be navigation rather than memory.
  • English-language sources only, and heavily weighted to one support forum.
  • Counts are of units, not people. One person posting twice counts twice.
